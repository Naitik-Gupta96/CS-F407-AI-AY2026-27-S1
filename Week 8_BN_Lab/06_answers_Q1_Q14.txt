BN LAB - ANSWERS TO QUESTIONS 1-14

Q1. Why is the chain-rule decomposition useful?
It breaks the probability of a complete sentence into next-token conditional
probabilities, so the model can generate a sentence one token at a time.

Q2. What independence assumption is made?
The first-order model assumes:
P(X_t | X_1, ..., X_{t-1}) ≈ P(X_t | X_{t-1})
So only the immediately previous token is used.

Q3. Conditional distributions:
P(next | the): cat=3/12=0.250, dog=3/12=0.250,
mat=2/12=0.167, rug=2/12=0.167, park=2/12=0.167.
P(next | cat): sat=2/3=0.667, ran=1/3=0.333.
P(next | dog): sat=2/3=0.667, ran=1/3=0.333.
P(next | sat): on=1.000.
P(next | ran): to=1.000.
Unseen transitions have probability 0.

Q4. Where are the counts stored?
In the nested structure counts[current][next].

Q5. Where is P(X_t | X_{t-1}) computed?
While building the probability table, each transition count is divided by
the total outgoing count for that current word.

Q6. How is the next word chosen?
Sampling chooses randomly according to the learned probabilities. Greedy
generation always chooses the largest-probability next word.

Q7. What happens for an unseen transition?
There is no learned distribution for that context, so the implementation
stops instead of inventing a transition.

Q8. What does a total of 0.87 mean?
The conditional distribution is not normalized. Some probability mass is
missing or the calculation is wrong.

Q9. Are model predictions always what a person expects?
No. The model only reflects patterns in this small dataset, not general
human language knowledge.

Q10. Greedy vs sampling:
Sampling gives more variation because it can choose different words according
to their probabilities. Greedy repeatedly follows the highest-probability
path. In this first-order model it can enter the cycle:
the -> cat -> sat -> on -> the.

Q11. First-order vs second-order:
First-order uses P(X_t | X_{t-1}); second-order uses
P(X_t | X_{t-2}, X_{t-1}). The second-order network has two incoming
dependencies for the predicted token, keeps larger CPTs, uses more context,
and generally needs more data.

Q12. Why does more context help but also need more data?
It gives the model more information for distinguishing contexts, but it
creates many more possible context combinations. With a small dataset,
many of those combinations are rare or unseen, so their probabilities are
harder to estimate.

Q13. Why is a specific LLM prompt preferable?
It tells the LLM exactly what probabilistic model, representation and tests
are required. That makes the generated code easier to inspect and validate.

Q14. What did the Bayesian-network view add?
It made word dependencies explicit, gave a factorization of the joint
distribution, provided a direct interpretation of conditional probabilities,
and gave a principled way to generate text and discuss independence
assumptions.
